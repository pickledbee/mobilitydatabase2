How the number works

The ADA guide allows a ramp to rise 1 inch for every 12 inches of length, written 1:12. So the ramp length in inches is the rise in inches times 12. Here are some examples at 1:12:

RiseRamp lengthRuns
6 inches6 ft1
12 inches12 ft1
18 inches18 ft1
24 inches24 ft1
30 inches30 ft1
36 inches36 ft2, with a landing between

Rules to know

  • Slope: 1:12 at most. Gentler is easier.
  • Rise per run: 30 inches at most. A taller rise needs a flat landing, then another run.
  • Width: at least 36 inches clear between handrails.
  • Landings: flat at the top and bottom of every run. At a turn, at least 60 by 60 inches.
  • Handrails: on both sides when the rise is more than 6 inches, extending at least 12 inches past the top and bottom.
  • Edge protection: a curb, rail or wide surface so a wheel cannot slip off the side.

These come from the federal ADA design guide for public buildings. Your home may follow different local rules, and some places need a permit. Check with your city, village or town building office before you build. Source: U.S. Access Board, ramps and curb ramps.

Before you buy or build

  • Ask an occupational therapist what slope the person using the ramp can manage.
  • Get at least two written quotes from licensed, insured contractors.
  • Think about rain, snow and ice. Wisconsin winters make a surface with grip and a gentler slope worth the extra room.
  • If you only need a ramp for a short time, ask about renting a modular ramp.

Planning tool, not advice. This page gives estimates only. It is not medical, legal, building or financial advice. An occupational therapist or doctor can tell you what equipment suits the person who will use it, and a licensed contractor can give you a real quote.
